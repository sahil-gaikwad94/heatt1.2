Welcome to this edition of our Tools for Thought series, where we interview founders on a mission to help us think better and work smarter. In this conversation we explored a deceptively simple idea: saving information is not the same as remembering it.

## The problem was never the capturing

I'm the kind of person who keeps lists of everything — to-do lists, someday-maybe lists, lists of things to remember. And it doesn't stop at tasks: movies I want to watch, recipes I want to try, books, places we might visit one day. Capturing things is how I close open loops. Once something is written down, my head can let it go.

The problem was never the capturing, it was where everything ended up. The movies lived in watchlists on three different streaming platforms. The recipes sat in chat threads. Interesting quotes and facts went into a notebook or onto sticky notes, and book takeaways into a note app. Every app is happy to hold its own little list. None of them talk to each other, and none of them care whether you ever come back.

But coming back is the whole point. A bookmark is a small promise to your future self: I will read this, cook this, remember this. Most tools let you break that promise silently. I'd finish a book full of ideas, write down the takeaways, and six months later I couldn't find the notes, let alone remember the ideas. Saving something feels productive in the moment, but saving is not remembering.

## Why internalizing knowledge still matters

With search engines and AI assistants one keystroke away, some people argue we don't need to remember anything anymore. But knowledge you carry is the only knowledge you can think with. Connections don't happen in a database, they happen in your head. You notice that an idea in an article contradicts something from a book you read last year, or that a concept from biology maps beautifully onto a problem at work. If neither of them is in your memory, no search engine will produce that spark for you.

There's a social side too. Everyone can look facts up now, which is exactly why carrying knowledge has become rarer and more interesting. The most interesting person in the room isn't the one who can search fastest. It's the person who can tell you the story behind the fact, connect it to what you just said, and pull in something surprising from a completely different field.

I actually think AI assistants make remembering more valuable, not less. When answers are cheap, what sets people apart is judgment, taste, and the things they really know. The assistants are great at retrieving. The connecting and the doubting still happen in a human head, and they work with whatever you've actually retained.

## Closing the loop

The loop worth caring about is simple: save something in two seconds, actually read it, connect it to what you know, and remember it. Most tools stop after the first step. Because the forgetting curve doesn't care where you learned something — read a brilliant article on Monday, and by Friday most of it is gone. That's just how memory works when you never revisit material.

That has always struck me as a waste. We put real effort into finding and reading good things, and then let almost all of it evaporate. Spaced repetition — bringing a note back right around the time you'd otherwise forget it — belongs not just in flashcard apps and exam prep, but around the knowledge we deliberately gather as adults. Reviewing your own material feels completely different from studying somebody else's deck. You picked every single card, so every question is about something you once cared enough to save.
