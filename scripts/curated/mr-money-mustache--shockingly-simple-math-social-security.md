In the world of very early retirement, we tend to ignore one of the most important things for normal retirees: the Social Security program.

Don't get me wrong, it's a great thing for the 73 million people who currently draw benefits, and it provides an essential safety net for many who don't have much income from anywhere else. But for those of us leaving the workplace in our 30s and 40s, Social Security is just a fuzzy concept in the very distant future.

There is an endless pile of conflicting opinions: Social Security is going to be bankrupt, so don't count on it. No, actually it will never be canceled, because old people vote. You should delay your withdrawals as long as possible to get the largest payments. No, take them as early as possible so you can retire earlier.

But when I did the math on all of this, I realized that it's more confusing than it needs to be. Because when it comes to deciding how Social Security fits into your retirement strategy, it really boils down to only one number: **the net present value of your future lifetime stream of Social Security payments.**

## The two extremes

If you want to retire in your 30s like I did, the value of your future Social Security is pretty close to zero. Even if the government keeps all of its promises, those payments are so far in the future that you need to save a full retirement 'stash to get through the three decades until it kicks in.

On the other hand, if you're already over 60, contributed throughout your career, and have a low cost of living, you may be eligible for payments that already meet all your needs. In this situation, you are set for life.

In between these two extremes, the math gets interesting. While most people assume Social Security is useless until you reach full eligibility, the real story is that you can think of it as a chunk of money that you already have right now, which reduces the amount of saving you have to do on your own.

## Running the numbers

Let's calculate the value a young couple might get at 30 years old. Assume a future Social Security income of $4000 per month, collected starting at 62 until age 92, and a compounding rate for investments of about 6% after inflation.

The net present value of that 30-year stream, if it started right now, is about $667,166. But you don't need the money until 32 years from now, so applying the discount a second time brings it down to around $98,280.

It means that if you stuck $98k into an investment account today and let it compound for 32 years, at that point it would be big enough to provide a stream of $4000 payments for the following 30 years — and this even takes future inflation into account.

## A more typical case

Consider Shane, who is 55. He has $3000 monthly expenses, $250k in investments, and no debt. Most advisors would tell him to keep working until at least 62. But let's do the math.

Using the online Social Security tool, his options might be $1968/month at age 62, $2796/month at 67, or $3467/month at 70. The net present value of each: about $210,433, $203,849, and $197,192 respectively.

Counterintuitively, it's actually less valuable to work longer for the higher benefits — for people who understand investing and the time value of money. If you took payments as early as possible and invested them at 6% after inflation, you'd build a lead that later, larger payments never catch up to.

## How to use this

The net effect of Social Security is that it should help you worry a little less and work a little less. In your early 30s, accounting for it will let you retire 1-2 years earlier. In your 60s, it's already here and can let you retire up to 30 years earlier, in the case that you can live on those payments alone.
